# Stage 0: Devanāgarī — Script Literacy

## Why this stage exists, and why it is optional

Every card in this course shows Sanskrit in Devanāgarī. A learner who cannot
yet decode that script does not meet Stage 1 as a vocabulary problem; they
meet it as a wall. This stage takes the wall down, and does nothing else.

It is optional because many people arrive already reading the script, and
for them it would be a hundred cards in the way. It counts towards no track's
percentage. Take it if you need it, skip it if you do not.

It is also deliberately **not** Varṇa-Vidyā. Stage 2 teaches where in the
mouth each sound is made, the five sthānas, the sparśa matrix and the
Maheśvara sūtras — that is phonology, and it is a different subject from
knowing which mark on the page says which sound. This stage asks only one question:
given this shape, what do I read?

## What a Devanāgarī word actually is

Devanāgarī is not an alphabet in the European sense and it is not a syllabary
either. It is an **abugida**: the unit that gets written is a consonant with a
vowel already attached to it.

Three consequences follow, and almost everything else in this stage is one of
them worked out.

**A consonant letter is not a bare consonant.** क is not k. It is ka. Every
consonant letter carries a short a inside it unless something on the page says
otherwise. This is the single fact that most often trips a beginner up, because
nothing is written for that a — it is there by default.

**A different vowel is written as a sign on the consonant, not as a letter.**
का is kā, कि is ki, कु is ku. The letter is the same letter throughout; what
changes is the mark hung on it. The independent letters आ इ उ are used only
where there is no consonant to hang the mark on — which in practice means at
the start of a word.

**No vowel at all has to be said explicitly.** Either a small stroke, the
virāma, is written under the consonant — क् is a bare k — or the consonant is
tied to the one after it in a conjunct, which says the same thing: nothing
comes between these two.

## The unit on the page

What you actually read across a line is not a letter and not a syllable but an
**orthographic syllable**: a consonant, or a cluster of consonants written as
one shape, plus whatever vowel sign and whatever mark above or after it belong
to that shape. निर्मलम् is four such units, not eight letters. Learning to see
those units — where one ends and the next begins — is what decoding is.

Two of them break the rule that the page runs left to right, and both are
worth meeting early:

- the i sign is written **before** the consonant it belongs to, and read
  after it. कि is ki, never ik.
- the repha, the little hook of र riding above a letter, is read **before**
  that letter. कर्म is karma, not kamra.

Neither is an exception you have to remember case by case. Both are one rule:
where a mark sits and when it is read are different questions.

## Conjuncts are a system, not a list

There are several hundred conjunct shapes in ordinary print, and there is no
point in learning them as a list. Almost all of them are made the same way:
the first consonant gives up its upright stroke and the next is written
against the stump. Once you can see that, न्त, स्थ, ल्प, ग्न and a few hundred
more read themselves.

What has to be learnt by sight is the handful that do not work that way — क्ष
and ज्ञ above all, where nothing of either parent survives; the stacked pairs
like त्त; the shapes श takes; and the two forms of र. Those are what this
stage cards. The rest is the rule.

## What you should be able to do at the end

Open a page of Sanskrit you have never seen and read it aloud, correctly,
without knowing what any of it means. That is the whole target. Meaning is
Stage 1's business, and grammar is everything after it; this stage is
finished when the script has stopped being the thing in the way.
