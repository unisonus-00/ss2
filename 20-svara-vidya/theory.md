# Svara-Vidyā — The Science of Vedic Accent

## Goal
Understand the three Vedic svaras (udātta, anudātta, svarita), their rules in the Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda tradition, and the difference between Vedic recitation and classical Sanskrit. Learn to mark svaras on a mantra. Understand the vikṛti system (jaṭā, ghana) as sonic architecture.

## Position in the Syllabus
Insert after Chandas I (syllable weight). You just learned laghu/guru — the *weight* dimension of the syllable. Svara is the *pitch* dimension. Classical Sanskrit lost accent; Vedic Sanskrit preserves it. This stage recovers what was lost.

## Why This Matters

The Taittirīya Upaniṣad opens the Śikṣāvallī with:

> *śīkṣāṃ vyākhyāsyāmaḥ — varṇaḥ svaraḥ mātrā balaṃ sāma santānaḥ*
> "We shall explain Śikṣā: letter, accent, measure, force, melody, junction."

Six factors govern sacred pronunciation. You already know **varṇa** (letter — from Varṇa-Vidyā) and **mātrā** (duration — from Chandas I). Now you learn **svara** — the factor the tradition considers most dangerous to get wrong.

---

## Part 1: The Three Svaras

Vedic Sanskrit is a **tonal language** — each syllable carries a pitch-accent that is part of the word's identity. Classical Sanskrit (post-Pāṇini) lost this tonal system; Vedic recitation preserves it.

| Svara | Sanskrit | Pitch | Marking (KYV) | What It Does |
|-------|----------|-------|---------------|-------------|
| **Udātta** | उदात्त | Raised / high | Unmarked (no sign) | The "lit" syllable — the peak of the word's pitch contour |
| **Anudātta** | अनुदात्त | Lowered / low | Underline (horizontal bar below) | The valley — the syllable before the rise |
| **Svarita** | स्वरित | Falling / compound | Vertical bar above | Immediately follows udātta — the pitch falls from the peak |

### The Basic Pattern

In most Vedic words, one syllable is **udātta** (high). The syllable immediately after it is **svarita** (falling). All other syllables are **anudātta** (low).

Example with **agním** (Agni, accusative):

```
a   gním
↓    ↑↘
anu  ud+sva
```

- **a** = anudātta (low) — marked with underline
- **gni** = udātta (high) — unmarked
- The pitch rises from "a" to "gni" and would fall on the next syllable if there were one

### The Indraśatru Warning

The tradition's most famous teaching on svara:

> *indraśatruḥ vardhasva*

With one svara pattern, this means "O Indra's enemy, grow!" — which is what the priest intended. But with wrong svara it becomes "May Indra's enemy grow!" — and the demon Vṛtra was killed by his own priest's mispronunciation.

The Taittirīya Brāhmaṇa records this story as a warning: **svara is not decoration. It is the direction of the mantra's power.** Wrong pitch does not make the mantra slightly less effective — it makes it a *different mantra* aimed at a different target.

## Part 2: Svara Rules in the Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda

The Taittirīya Prātiśākhya (the phonetics manual specific to the KYV) provides the exact rules for svara behavior. Key principles:

### Rule 1: Every Word Has One Udātta

Each independent word has exactly one udātta syllable (with rare exceptions for compounds). The udātta is inherent to the word — it is part of the word's identity, not a choice made by the reciter.

### Rule 2: Svarita Follows Udātta

The syllable immediately after an udātta becomes svarita — the pitch falls. This is called **jātya svarita** (natural svarita) when it occurs within a word.

### Rule 3: Anudātta Fills Everything Else

All syllables that are neither udātta nor svarita are anudātta — the default low pitch.

### Rule 4: Sandhi Affects Svara

When words combine in saṃhitā-pāṭha (continuous recitation), the svara pattern of the combined sequence may differ from the individual words. The Prātiśākhya specifies exactly how svaras interact at word-boundaries:

- When two udāttas would be adjacent, rules determine which survives
- Svarita can be **enclitic** (kampa-svarita) — a wavering pitch that signals the meeting of two tonal contours
- Certain particles (like ca, tu, hi) lose their accent and become enclitic

### Rule 5: Verbs in Main Clauses Lose Accent

In main clauses (not subordinate), finite verbs become anudātta — they lose their natural udātta. This is why verb-accent is often different in Vedic prose (brāhmaṇa) vs Vedic hymns (mantra).

## Part 3: The Marking System

The Kṛṣṇa Yajurveda uses a specific notation:

| What | Mark | Position | Example |
|------|------|----------|---------|
| **Anudātta** | Horizontal line | Below the syllable | a̱gne (the "a" is low) |
| **Udātta** | (No mark) | — | The unmarked syllable is the peak |
| **Svarita** | Vertical line | Above the syllable | The syllable after udātta |

This is the opposite of what you might expect — the "special" syllable (udātta) is unmarked, while the "default" (anudātta) is marked. The logic: if you know where the anudātta and svarita are, the udātta is everything else. The system marks the *support structure,* not the peak.

### Reading Example: First Line of the Namakam

> *namaḥ somāya ca rudrāya ca*

Svara pattern (simplified):
```
na  maḥ  so  mā  ya  ca  rud rā  ya  ca
↓   ↓    ↑   ↘   ↓   ↓   ↑   ↘   ↓   ↓
an  an   ud  sv   an  an  ud  sv   an  an
```

- **so** in somāya carries the udātta — the word peaks there
- **mā** immediately after is svarita — the pitch falls
- **rud** in rudrāya carries the udātta
- **rā** is svarita
- Everything else is anudātta

### Practice: Marking Svaras

To mark svaras on a mantra you know:
1. Identify each word's natural udātta (from a pada-pāṭha text or teacher)
2. Mark the syllable after each udātta as svarita
3. Mark everything else as anudātta
4. Check sandhi-boundaries for svara interactions

## Part 4: Saṃhitā vs Pada — The Sandhi Dimension

The KYV tradition preserves every mantra in (at least) two forms:

| Form | What It Is | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| **Saṃhitā-pāṭha** | Continuous recitation with full sandhi | *namo̍ rudra̱ya ca* (sounds flow together) |
| **Pada-pāṭha** | Word-by-word with sandhi dissolved | *namaḥ · rudrāya · ca* (each word isolated) |

### Why Both Exist

- **Saṃhitā** is how the mantra is *performed* — it is the living, breathing form. Sandhi creates the continuous sound-stream.
- **Pada** is how the mantra is *analyzed* — each word is separated so you can see the grammar. Where saṃhitā says "namo" (namaḥ + r → namo), pada shows you the original "namaḥ" + "rudrāya."

The relationship between saṃhitā and pada is the relationship between *performance and analysis,* between the woven cloth and the individual threads.

### Svara in Saṃhitā vs Pada

Crucially, the svaras in saṃhitā-pāṭha are not always identical to those in pada-pāṭha. When words join (sandhi), their svara contours interact. The Prātiśākhya specifies the resulting combined pattern. Learning to hear the difference — to hear the individual word-accents underneath the continuous flow — is a major step in Vedic literacy.

## Part 5: The Vikṛtis — Weaving the Sound-Body

Beyond simple recitation, the Vedic tradition developed eight **vikṛti** patterns — systematic rearrangements of the word-order that weave the text forward and backward:

### The Hierarchy of Recitation

| Level | Name | Pattern (for words A B C D) | Complexity |
|-------|------|----------------------------|-----------|
| 1 | **Saṃhitā** | A B C D | Continuous, linear |
| 2 | **Pada** | A \| B \| C \| D | Word-by-word, no sandhi |
| 3 | **Krama** | AB, BC, CD | Pairs, stepping forward |
| 4 | **Jaṭā** | AB BA AB, BC CB BC, CD DC CD | Forward-backward-forward |
| 5 | **Ghana** | AB BA ABC, BC CB BCD, CD DC CDE | Triple weave |

### Krama-Pāṭha (Step Recitation)

The first step beyond pada. Take the words in overlapping pairs:

For *namaḥ somāya ca rudrāya ca:*
```
namaḥ somāya    (words 1-2)
somāya ca       (words 2-3)
ca rudrāya      (words 3-4)
rudrāya ca      (words 4-5)
```

Each word appears twice — once as the second member of a pair, once as the first of the next pair. This overlap ensures that if any word were to be lost, its neighbor preserves the link.

### Jaṭā-Pāṭha (Braid Recitation)

Forward-backward-forward for each pair:

```
namaḥ somāya — somāya namaḥ — namaḥ somāya
somāya ca — ca somāya — somāya ca
ca rudrāya — rudrāya ca — ca rudrāya
rudrāya ca — ca rudrāya — rudrāya ca
```

The name "jaṭā" (matted locks) describes what happens to the sound — words interlock like the matted hair of an ascetic. You cannot remove one strand without disturbing the whole.

### Ghana-Pāṭha (Dense/Block Recitation)

The most complex standard vikṛti:

```
namaḥ somāya — somāya namaḥ — namaḥ somāya ca — ca somāya namaḥ — namaḥ somāya ca
somāya ca — ca somāya — somāya ca rudrāya — rudrāya ca somāya — somāya ca rudrāya
...
```

The pattern: AB BA ABC CBA ABC, then shift.

Ghana-pāṭha produces a **three-dimensional weave** — forward movement (sṛṣṭi), backward movement (saṃhāra), and the compound three-word movement (sthiti). When you hear Chamaka-ghana, you are hearing the Chamakam woven into a sonic sculpture.

### Why the Vikṛtis Exist

1. **Preservation:** The overlapping patterns make corruption nearly impossible. If a single word changes, the error is immediately obvious because it breaks the forward AND backward patterns. The Vedic text has been preserved with extraordinary fidelity for 3,000+ years — largely because of this system.

2. **Sonic architecture:** Ghana-pāṭha creates overtone patterns and resonances impossible in linear recitation. The weaving produces a quality of sound that is qualitatively different from simple chanting.

3. **Dhāraṇā training:** The reciter must hold the entire sequence in mind while weaving forward and backward. This is high-order memory training — directly relevant to the avadhāna skills you will develop in later stages of this syllabus.

## Part 6: Svara vs Chandas — Two Dimensions of the Syllable

You now know two systems that operate on the same syllable:

| System | What It Measures | Values | Where Used |
|--------|-----------------|--------|-----------|
| **Chandas** (meter) | Syllable *weight* | Laghu (light) / guru (heavy) | Classical + Vedic poetry |
| **Svara** (accent) | Syllable *pitch* | Udātta (high) / anudātta (low) / svarita (falling) | Vedic recitation only |

A syllable can be:
- laghu AND udātta (light AND high-pitched)
- guru AND anudātta (heavy AND low-pitched)
- Any combination — the two systems are independent

Classical Sanskrit poetry retained chandas but lost svara — syllable weight still matters (for anuṣṭubh, śārdūlavikrīḍita, etc.) but pitch-accent is gone. The Vedic tradition preserves both.

### Why Classical Sanskrit Lost Svara

Pāṇini's grammar records the accent system but notes that it was already becoming archaic in everyday speech. The Prātiśākhya texts preserve the rules because the Vedic recitation tradition *needed* them — the mantras required exact pitch. But laukika (worldly) Sanskrit moved toward a stress-based system instead of a pitch-based one.

This means: when you compose a śloka in classical Sanskrit (stages 12-22 of this syllabus), you control syllable *weight* through meter. When you recite a Vedic mantra, you control syllable *pitch* through svara. The two disciplines use different parameters on the same raw material.

## Part 7: The Six Factors — Complete Framework

Returning to the Taittirīya Upaniṣad's framework, you now know four of the six factors:

| Factor | What | Where You Learned It |
|--------|------|---------------------|
| **Varṇa** (letter) | Which sound | Varṇa-Vidyā: sthāna, prayatna, the 48 letters |
| **Svara** (accent) | Which pitch | This stage: udātta, anudātta, svarita |
| **Mātrā** (duration) | How long | Chandas I: laghu (1 mātrā), guru (2 mātrā), pluta (3 mātrā) |
| **Bala** (force) | How much prāṇa | Practice-based: the pressure of articulation |
| **Sāma** (melody) | How smooth | Practice-based: evenness of flow between sounds |
| **Santāna** (junction) | How sounds connect | Sandhi rules + saṃhitā/pada distinction |

A fully competent Vedic reciter controls all six simultaneously. Each syllable is produced with the right letter, at the right pitch, for the right duration, with the right force, connected smoothly to its neighbors. This is the standard the tradition sets.
